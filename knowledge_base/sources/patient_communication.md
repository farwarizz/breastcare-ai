# Patient Communication in Breast Cancer Screening

## Communicating Risk

Risk communication is a critical skill in breast cancer screening. Patients must understand their risk level without undue alarm or false reassurance. Use absolute risk numbers rather than relative risk. For example, say "8 out of 100 women with similar features develop breast cancer" rather than "your risk is doubled."

## Conveying Screening Results

**Normal results**: Explain that a normal mammogram is reassuring but does not guarantee the absence of cancer. Mammography detects approximately 80–90% of breast cancers; some cancers may be missed, particularly in dense breast tissue. Encourage continued screening at recommended intervals.

**Abnormal results**: Most abnormal mammograms do not indicate cancer. Approximately 10% of screening mammograms result in recall for additional imaging. Of those recalled, approximately 80–90% are found to be benign after additional workup. Explain that additional testing is a routine part of the screening process, not a diagnosis.

**BI-RADS 3 (probably benign)**: This result carries less than 2% probability of malignancy. Recommend short-interval follow-up (typically 6 months). Explain that this approach avoids unnecessary biopsy while maintaining safety. Emphasize the importance of completing the follow-up schedule.

**BI-RADS 4 (suspicious)**: Biopsy will likely be recommended. Explain the range of suspicion (4A, 4B, 4C) and approximate probability. Approximately 70% of biopsies for BI-RADS 4 findings return benign results. Discuss the biopsy procedure (typically core needle biopsy under image guidance).

**BI-RADS 5 (highly suspicious)**: The probability of malignancy exceeds 95%. Biopsy is required. Prepare the patient for a likely cancer diagnosis while maintaining hope about treatment options.

## Psychological Impact

Breast cancer screening findings can cause significant anxiety. False-positive mammograms cause measurable psychological distress that persists for up to 3 years in some women. Women who receive a recall notice experience anxiety levels comparable to those diagnosed with cancer, even when the finding is ultimately benign. Clear, timely communication reduces anxiety.

## Best Practices for Clinicians

Use plain language: avoid medical jargon when possible. Provide written materials to supplement verbal explanations. Allow time for questions and express empathy. Use visual aids (diagrams, probability charts) to explain risk. Frame information positively when appropriate: "this means an 85% chance the finding is benign." Acknowledge uncertainty explicitly rather than providing false certainty. Refer to support resources (patient navigators, counseling) when appropriate.

## Cultural Sensitivity

Screening participation and risk perception vary across cultural backgrounds. Language barriers affect comprehension of screening results. Some cultures may have specific beliefs about cancer that influence willingness to screen or pursue treatment. Use culturally appropriate communication strategies and professional interpreters when needed.

## Decision-Making Frameworks

Shared decision-making: the clinician presents evidence-based options and the patient makes an informed choice aligned with their values. This is particularly important for: screening initiation age (40 vs. 50), screening frequency (annual vs. biennial), management of BI-RADS 3 findings (follow-up vs. biopsy), and treatment choices for early-stage cancers.
