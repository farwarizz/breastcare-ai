# Patient Communication in Breast Cancer Screening

## Communicating Risk

Patients need to understand their risk without undue alarm or false reassurance. Use absolute risks and natural frequencies rather than relative risks: "about 8 out of 100 women with this finding turn out to have cancer" is clearer than "your risk is doubled", which says nothing about the starting point. State the time frame the risk refers to, and say what the number is based on.

## Explaining a Model's Risk Estimate

An AI risk estimate is a statistic, not a diagnosis. A probability of 40% means that, among masses with the same descriptors in the data the model learned from, about 40 in 100 were malignant. The data behind this tool came from one institution's diagnostic cases, nearly half of which were malignant, so its probabilities are not screening-population rates. A prediction set that contains both "benign" and "malignant" means the model cannot separate the two for these inputs. Whatever the estimate, the next step is set by the radiologist's assessment and, where indicated, a biopsy.

## Conveying Screening Results

**Normal results**: A normal mammogram is reassuring but does not rule out cancer. Mammography finds roughly 80–87% of cancers present, and fewer in dense breasts. Encourage continued screening at the recommended interval and prompt review of any new symptom.

**Abnormal results**: Most abnormal screening mammograms are not cancer. About 10% of US screening mammograms lead to a recall for more imaging, and only about one in twenty recalled women has cancer. Explain that additional views or an ultrasound are a routine part of screening, not a diagnosis.

**BI-RADS 3 (probably benign)**: The chance of malignancy is 2% or less. Short-interval follow-up, usually at six months, avoids a biopsy for most women while keeping them safe. Stress that completing the follow-up schedule is what makes this approach safe.

**BI-RADS 4 (suspicious)**: A biopsy will usually be recommended. Explain the level of suspicion (4A, 4B or 4C) and the approximate probability. In US benchmark data, about 29% of biopsies prompted by screening find cancer, so roughly seven in ten are benign. Describe the biopsy procedure, usually an image-guided core needle biopsy.

**BI-RADS 5 (highly suggestive of malignancy)**: The probability of malignancy is 95% or more and a biopsy is required. Prepare the patient for a probable cancer diagnosis while explaining that treatment planning follows the biopsy result.

## Psychological Impact

Screening results cause measurable distress. In a Danish cohort study, six months after a false-positive mammogram women reported changes in existential values and inner calmness as great as women diagnosed with breast cancer, and three years later they still reported more negative psychosocial consequences than women with a normal result. Clear, timely communication and a short wait for the follow-up test reduce this harm.

## Best Practices for Clinicians

Use plain language and avoid jargon. Give written material to supplement the conversation. Allow time for questions and acknowledge emotion. Use visual aids such as icon arrays to show probabilities. Frame numbers both ways when appropriate: "about 85 in 100 findings like this are benign" alongside "about 15 in 100 are cancer". Acknowledge uncertainty rather than offering false certainty, and refer to patient navigators or counselling when needed.

## Cultural Sensitivity

Screening participation and the perception of risk vary across cultural backgrounds, and language barriers affect understanding of results. Beliefs about cancer can influence willingness to screen or to pursue treatment. Use culturally appropriate materials and professional interpreters rather than family members when needed.

## Common Misconceptions

**"Mammograms cause breast cancer."** A screening mammogram uses a small radiation dose, about 0.28 mSv (0.34 mSv for tomosynthesis), roughly a month of natural background radiation. The National Cancer Institute estimates that, in theory, annual mammograms between ages 40 and 80 could cause up to one breast cancer per 1,000 women. That theoretical risk is weighed against the reduction in breast cancer deaths that screening trials have shown, and it is not a reason to avoid recommended screening.

**"A biopsy spreads the cancer."** A core needle biopsy can displace tumour cells along the needle track. A systematic review found displaced cells in the surgical specimens of about 22% of patients, more often when surgery followed the biopsy quickly, but no study reported an increase in local recurrence, and the one study that examined survival found no worse survival after a needle biopsy. A biopsy is how a suspicious finding is diagnosed, and forgoing it carries the far larger risk of leaving a cancer untreated.

**"A normal mammogram rules out cancer."** Mammography finds roughly 80–87% of the cancers present, and fewer in dense breasts. A new lump or change in the breast needs assessment even after a recent normal screening mammogram.

**"A high AI estimate means I have cancer."** A model's probability describes how often masses with similar descriptors were malignant in its training data. It is not a diagnosis, and only a biopsy reviewed by a pathologist can confirm or exclude cancer.

**"A low AI estimate means it is benign."** A low estimate means that masses with similar descriptors were usually benign in the training data. It does not rule out cancer for any individual: the radiologist's assessment decides the next step, and only a biopsy can confirm or exclude cancer.

**"Antiperspirants or deodorants cause breast cancer."** The National Cancer Institute states that no scientific evidence links these products to the development of breast cancer, and the American Cancer Society lists antiperspirants among disproven or controversial risk factors.

**"Wearing a bra, including an underwire bra, causes breast cancer."** The American Cancer Society states that there are no scientific or clinical data showing that wearing a bra changes the risk of breast cancer.

**"No one in my family had breast cancer, so this cannot be cancer."** More than 85% of women diagnosed with breast cancer have no family history of the disease, according to the American Cancer Society, and only up to about 10% of breast cancers are thought to be hereditary. A missing family history does not rule cancer out.

**"Dense breasts are a disease."** The National Cancer Institute states that dense breasts are not an abnormal breast condition or a disease. They are common (found in nearly half of women aged 40 and over who have mammograms), but they do raise breast cancer risk and make mammograms harder to read.

**"If a lump does not hurt, it cannot be cancer."** A painless, hard, fixed mass with irregular edges is more likely to be cancer, but breast cancer can also be soft, round, tender or even painful, according to the American Cancer Society. Pain neither rules cancer in nor out.

## Decision-Making Frameworks

In shared decision-making the clinician presents the evidence-based options and the patient chooses according to her values. It matters most where guidelines differ or the options are close: when to start screening (40 or 45) and how often (annually or every two years), whether to add supplemental imaging for dense breasts, whether to follow up or biopsy a probably benign finding, and the choice between breast-conserving surgery and mastectomy for early cancers.
