# Breast Cancer Treatment Pathways

## Overview

Breast cancer treatment is multimodal and individualised. The pathway depends on the stage, the tumour's biology (hormone-receptor and HER2 status, grade, and sometimes a genomic test), and the patient's age, other illnesses and preferences. Decisions are made by a multidisciplinary team; this document describes the main building blocks, not a recommendation for any individual.

## Treatment Modalities

### Surgery

**Breast-conserving surgery (lumpectomy)**: Removes the tumour with a rim of normal tissue and is usually followed by radiation therapy. For appropriately selected early-stage cancers it gives the same survival as mastectomy.

**Mastectomy**: Removes the whole breast. It is chosen when the tumour is large relative to the breast, when there are several tumours in different parts of the breast, after previous breast radiation, or by patient preference. Variants include skin-sparing and nipple-sparing mastectomy with reconstruction.

**Sentinel lymph node biopsy**: Removes the first lymph node(s) draining the breast to check for spread. When the sentinel nodes are clear, a full axillary dissection is avoided, which lowers the risk of lymphoedema.

### Radiation Therapy

**Whole-breast radiation**: Standard after breast-conserving surgery. In the pooled randomised trials, radiation roughly halved the ten-year risk of recurrence and reduced breast cancer deaths by about one-sixth. Hypofractionated schedules of three to four weeks are standard, and a one-week, five-fraction schedule was non-inferior in a large UK trial.

**Partial-breast irradiation**: Treats only the area around the tumour bed over a shorter course, for selected low-risk patients.

**Post-mastectomy radiation**: Recommended when four or more lymph nodes are involved, the tumour is larger than 5 cm, or the margins are positive, and considered when one to three nodes are involved. It reduces chest-wall recurrence and, in node-positive disease, breast cancer deaths.

### Systemic Therapy

**Chemotherapy**: Given before surgery (neoadjuvant) or after it (adjuvant). Common regimens are AC-T (doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide followed by paclitaxel) and TC (docetaxel and cyclophosphamide). Neoadjuvant chemotherapy can shrink a tumour enough to allow breast conservation, and is standard for stage II–III triple-negative and HER2-positive cancers.

**Endocrine (hormonal) therapy**: For hormone receptor-positive cancers, which make up roughly 70–80% of breast cancers. Tamoxifen can be used before or after the menopause; aromatase inhibitors (anastrozole, letrozole, exemestane) are used after the menopause, or before it together with ovarian suppression. Treatment lasts at least five years and up to ten for higher-risk disease.

**Targeted therapy**: HER2-positive cancers (about 15–20%) are treated with trastuzumab, often with pertuzumab, and with the antibody–drug conjugates T-DM1 and trastuzumab deruxtecan. CDK4/6 inhibitors (palbociclib, ribociclib, abemaciclib) are standard with endocrine therapy for advanced hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative disease, and abemaciclib and ribociclib are also used for high-risk early disease. PARP inhibitors (olaparib, talazoparib) are used for HER2-negative cancers in carriers of a germline BRCA mutation, including olaparib after surgery for high-risk early cancers.

**Immunotherapy**: Pembrolizumab with chemotherapy is used for early-stage triple-negative breast cancer (before and after surgery, regardless of PD-L1 status) and for PD-L1-positive metastatic triple-negative disease.

## Treatment by Stage

**Stage 0 (ductal carcinoma in situ)**: Breast-conserving surgery with radiation, or mastectomy. Endocrine therapy may be offered for oestrogen receptor-positive DCIS to lower the risk of a new cancer. Chemotherapy is not used.

**Stage I**: Breast-conserving surgery with radiation (preferred) or mastectomy, with sentinel node biopsy. Systemic therapy depends on receptor status and, for hormone receptor-positive HER2-negative cancers, often on a genomic test.

**Stage II**: Surgery is chosen according to tumour and breast size, with axillary staging. Neoadjuvant therapy is often used, both to allow breast conservation and, in triple-negative and HER2-positive disease, to tailor treatment after surgery to the response.

**Stage III (locally advanced)**: Neoadjuvant systemic therapy to shrink the tumour, then surgery and radiation, with further systemic treatment as indicated.

**Stage IV (metastatic)**: The goal is to control the disease and preserve quality of life for as long as possible; cure is rare. Treatment is systemic (endocrine, targeted, chemotherapy or immunotherapy, according to tumour biology), with radiation or surgery for specific symptoms.

## Genomic Testing

**Oncotype DX**: A 21-gene recurrence score for hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative early breast cancer. In the TAILORx trial, women with scores of 0–10 did well with endocrine therapy alone, and for scores of 11–25 endocrine therapy alone was not inferior to adding chemotherapy overall, although women aged 50 or younger with scores of 16–25 gained some benefit from chemotherapy. Scores of 26 or above indicate a likely chemotherapy benefit.

**MammaPrint**: A 70-gene signature classifying tumours as low or high genomic risk. In the MINDACT trial, women at high clinical risk but low genomic risk had good outcomes without chemotherapy.
