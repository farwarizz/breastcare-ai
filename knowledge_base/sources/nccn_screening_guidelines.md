# Breast Cancer Screening Guidelines — NCCN, ACS and USPSTF

## Overview

Three US bodies publish widely used breast cancer screening guidelines: the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), the American Cancer Society (ACS) and the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF). They agree that average-risk women should have regular screening mammography from their forties and that women at high risk need earlier and more intensive screening, but they differ on the starting age, the interval and the role of supplemental imaging.

## NCCN Average-Risk Recommendations

NCCN (Breast Cancer Screening and Diagnosis, version 1.2026) defines average risk as no known genetic predisposition and no family or personal history suggesting increased risk.

**Ages 25–39**: A clinical encounter every one to three years that includes breast cancer risk assessment and a breast examination. Mammography is not recommended routinely.

**Age 40 and over**: An annual clinical encounter with ongoing risk assessment and a breast examination, plus an annual screening mammogram with tomosynthesis.

**Upper age limit**: There is none. Screening continues as long as the woman is healthy and expected to live more than ten years, unless she and her clinician decide otherwise.

## NCCN Increased-Risk Recommendations

NCCN recommends a breast cancer risk assessment by age 25. Women in any of the following groups see their clinician every six to twelve months and follow the schedule for their group.

**Residual lifetime risk of 20% or more** (calculated with a model based on comprehensive family history, such as BRCAPRO, Tyrer-Cuzick, BOADICEA/CanRisk or the BCSC model): annual mammography with tomosynthesis may begin as early as 30 and no later than 40; annual breast MRI may begin as early as 25 and no later than 40.

**Radiation therapy to the chest or breast between ages 10 and 30**: screening starts eight years after the radiation ended, but not before 25, with an annual mammogram with tomosynthesis and an annual breast MRI.

**Five-year risk of invasive breast cancer of 1.7% or more** (Gail model at age 35 or over, or an imaging-based model): annual mammography with tomosynthesis and consideration of annual MRI.

**Atypical ductal hyperplasia, lobular carcinoma in situ or atypical lobular hyperplasia**: annual mammography from diagnosis but not before 30, and consideration of annual MRI from diagnosis but not before 25.

**Dense breasts with no other risk factor**: annual mammography with tomosynthesis starting no later than 40 (not before 30). For heterogeneously dense breasts, supplemental tests are discussed individually; for extremely dense breasts, breast MRI is recommended from age 50 and possibly from 40.

When MRI is unavailable or cannot be done, contrast-enhanced mammography, molecular breast imaging or whole-breast ultrasound are alternatives.

## Genetic Risk Factors

A personal or family history suggesting a hereditary predisposition (for example BRCA1, BRCA2, TP53, PTEN, CDH1, PALB2, ATM or CHEK2) leads to referral for genetic counselling and testing, and screening then follows NCCN's guideline for hereditary breast, ovarian, pancreatic and prostate cancers. In general, women at increased risk may start annual mammography at 30 and annual MRI at 25. BRCA1 carriers have a 55–72% lifetime risk of breast cancer and BRCA2 carriers 45–69%.

## ACS Recommendations

For average-risk women, the ACS recommends that women aged 40–44 have the option to start annual mammography; women aged 45–54 have a mammogram every year; and women 55 and older switch to every other year or continue yearly, for as long as they are in good health and expected to live at least ten more years.

For high-risk women, the ACS recommends an annual breast MRI and mammogram, typically from age 30, for those with a lifetime risk of about 20–25% or greater by a family-history model, a known BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutation, a first-degree relative with such a mutation, chest radiation before age 30, or a hereditary syndrome such as Li-Fraumeni or Cowden syndrome. The ACS recommends against MRI screening when lifetime risk is below 15%.

## USPSTF Recommendations

The 2024 USPSTF recommendation is biennial screening mammography for all women aged 40–74 (grade B). It finds the evidence insufficient to assess screening in women 75 and older, and insufficient to recommend for or against supplemental ultrasound or MRI for women with dense breasts and an otherwise negative mammogram.

## Risk Assessment Tools

**Gail model (NCI Breast Cancer Risk Assessment Tool)**: Estimates five-year and lifetime risk of invasive breast cancer from age, age at first period, age at first live birth, the number of first-degree relatives with breast cancer, the number of previous breast biopsies and any atypical hyperplasia, with adjustment for race and ethnicity. It was developed in women aged 35–74 and should not be used for known mutation carriers, women with a history of breast cancer, DCIS or LCIS, or women who had chest radiation; it also does not use second-degree relatives or the age at which relatives were diagnosed.

**Tyrer-Cuzick (IBIS) model**: Uses a comprehensive family history on both sides of the family together with hormonal and reproductive factors, body mass index and, in recent versions, mammographic density. NCCN lists it among the models that can define a 20% lifetime risk.

**BRCAPRO and BOADICEA/CanRisk**: Estimate the probability of carrying a BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutation and the resulting cancer risks from the family history of breast and ovarian cancer.

**BCSC model**: Combines age, race and ethnicity, family history, previous biopsies and mammographic density to estimate five-year risk.
