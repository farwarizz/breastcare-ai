# Breast Imaging Modalities Compared

## Mammography

### How Mammography Works

A mammogram is a low-dose X-ray of the compressed breast, taken in two views of each breast. Full-field digital mammography records the image electronically. Digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT, "3D mammography") takes several low-dose exposures from different angles and reconstructs thin slices, which reduces the overlap of tissue that hides or mimics masses on a 2D image.

### Mammography Accuracy

In US screening data, mammography finds roughly 80–87% of the cancers present (sensitivity), with a specificity of about 89%. Sensitivity is lower in dense breasts, by 10–29 percentage points across all ages. About 10% of screened women are recalled for more tests, about 5 cancers are found per 1,000 screens, and only about 4–5% of recalled women turn out to have cancer. Over ten years of annual screening, about half of US women have at least one false-positive result, and 7–17% of these have a biopsy.

### Tomosynthesis

In a large US study comparing practices before and after adopting DBT, adding tomosynthesis lowered the recall rate from 107 to 91 per 1,000 screens and raised the cancer detection rate from 4.2 to 5.4 per 1,000, with most of the gain in invasive cancers. Whether DBT reduces breast cancer deaths compared with 2D mammography is not yet known and is being tested in the TMIST trial. NCCN recommends screening mammography with tomosynthesis.

### Mammography Strengths

Mammography is the only screening test shown in randomised trials to reduce breast cancer mortality. It is widely available, standardised through BI-RADS, and detects microcalcifications, an early sign of ductal carcinoma in situ that other modalities often miss.

### Mammography Limitations

It uses ionising radiation, although the dose is small: about 0.28 mSv for a screening digital mammogram and 0.34 mSv for tomosynthesis, equivalent to roughly one to one and a half months of natural background radiation. The National Cancer Institute estimates that, in theory, annual mammograms between ages 40 and 80 could cause up to one breast cancer per 1,000 women, a risk weighed against the deaths that screening prevents. Sensitivity falls in dense breasts, compression is uncomfortable for some women, and overlapping tissue on 2D images causes both missed cancers and false recalls.

## Breast Ultrasound

### How Ultrasound Works

Ultrasound uses high-frequency sound waves to produce real-time images without radiation. It distinguishes fluid-filled cysts from solid masses and shows the margins, shape and orientation of a mass.

### Ultrasound as Supplemental Screening

In women with dense breasts, adding screening ultrasound to mammography finds additional cancers but at the cost of many more false positives. In the ACRIN 6666 trial, ultrasound found 4.2 extra cancers per 1,000 women in the first round, while only 8.9% of the biopsies it prompted showed cancer, compared with 22.6% for mammography. In routine US practice the extra yield was smaller, about 1.3 cancers per 1,000 in the first year. The USPSTF finds the evidence insufficient to recommend for or against supplemental ultrasound or MRI for dense breasts alone.

### Ultrasound Strengths and Limitations

Ultrasound involves no radiation, is safe in pregnancy, guides needle biopsies in real time and is the first test for a palpable lump in women under 30. It cannot reliably show microcalcifications, its quality depends on the operator, and its false-positive rate is higher than mammography's.

## Breast MRI

### How Breast MRI Works

Breast MRI uses a magnetic field and radio waves with an intravenous gadolinium contrast agent. Cancers are detected mainly by their contrast enhancement, reflecting their blood supply, together with their morphology.

### MRI Accuracy

MRI is the most sensitive breast imaging test and detects cancers missed by mammography and ultrasound, particularly in dense breasts and in high-risk women. Its specificity is lower, so it leads to more recalls and biopsies. In the Dutch DENSE trial, offering MRI to women with extremely dense breasts after a normal mammogram cut the rate of interval cancers from 5.0 to 2.5 per 1,000 women invited (0.8 per 1,000 among those actually screened), at the cost of about 80 false-positive results per 1,000 MRI screens.

### When MRI Is Recommended

Annual MRI alongside mammography is recommended for women at high risk: a lifetime risk of about 20–25% or greater by a family-history model, a BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutation (or an untested first-degree relative of a carrier), or chest radiation before age 30. Under NCCN guidance, MRI for women at increased risk may begin at 25, and for women with extremely dense breasts and no other risk factor it can be considered from age 50 (possibly 40). MRI is also used to assess the extent of a known cancer before surgery, to monitor the response to neoadjuvant therapy and to check implants.

### MRI Limitations

MRI is expensive, takes longer than mammography, requires an intravenous contrast agent, and is not suitable for everyone (for example because of some implanted devices, severe claustrophobia or pregnancy). It does not show microcalcifications. Contrast-enhanced mammography, molecular breast imaging or whole-breast ultrasound are alternatives when MRI is unavailable or cannot be done.

## Comparison Summary

For routine screening, mammography (preferably with tomosynthesis) is the standard. For women at high risk, annual MRI is added. For dense breasts without other risk factors, supplemental ultrasound or MRI finds more cancers but with more false positives, and guidelines differ on whether to recommend it. For a palpable lump in a young woman, ultrasound is the first test. Imaging findings are described with the BI-RADS lexicon, and an assessment category (0–6) sets the follow-up.
