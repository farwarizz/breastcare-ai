# Breast Imaging Modalities Comparison

## Mammography

**Technology**: Low-dose X-ray imaging of compressed breast tissue. Available as full-field digital mammography (FFDM) and digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT/3D mammography).

**Sensitivity**: 77–95% overall. Drops to 48–64% in extremely dense breasts (ACR category D). DBT improves cancer detection rate by 27–53% compared to FFDM.

**Specificity**: 90–95% for FFDM. DBT reduces false-positive recalls by 15–17%.

**Strengths**: Only imaging modality proven to reduce breast cancer mortality in randomized controlled trials. Widely available, standardized (BI-RADS), relatively inexpensive. Detects microcalcifications (early sign of DCIS) that other modalities may miss.

**Limitations**: Ionizing radiation (low dose: approximately 0.4 mSv per standard 2-view exam). Reduced sensitivity in dense breasts. Uncomfortable due to breast compression. 2D mammography has tissue overlap; DBT reduces but does not eliminate this.

**BI-RADS Assessment Categories**: Category 0: Incomplete — additional imaging needed. Category 1: Negative — routine screening. Category 2: Benign — routine screening. Category 3: Probably benign — short-interval follow-up (6 months). Less than 2% malignancy risk. Category 4: Suspicious — biopsy should be considered. 4A: Low suspicion (2–10% risk). 4B: Moderate suspicion (10–50% risk). 4C: High suspicion (50–95% risk). Category 5: Highly suggestive of malignancy — biopsy required. Greater than 95% probability. Category 6: Known biopsy-proven malignancy.

## Breast Ultrasound

**Technology**: High-frequency sound waves (7–18 MHz) to produce real-time images without radiation.

**Sensitivity**: As supplemental screening in dense breasts: detects an additional 2–4 cancers per 1,000 women not seen on mammography.

**Specificity**: Lower than mammography — higher false-positive rate. Operator-dependent (skill and experience affect quality).

**Strengths**: No ionizing radiation. Real-time imaging — can guide biopsies. Distinguishes solid masses from cysts (fluid-filled structures). Safe during pregnancy. Widely available and relatively inexpensive.

**Limitations**: Operator-dependent — high variability between technologists. Cannot reliably detect microcalcifications. Higher false-positive rate than mammography alone. Automated breast ultrasound (ABUS) reduces operator dependence but increases interpretation time.

**When recommended**: Evaluating palpable findings in women under 30. Characterizing mammographically detected masses (solid vs. cystic). Supplemental screening for women with dense breasts. Guiding core needle biopsy or fine needle aspiration.

## Breast MRI

**Technology**: Magnetic resonance imaging with gadolinium contrast enhancement. Evaluates tissue vascularity (contrast uptake kinetics) and morphology.

**Sensitivity**: 93–100% (highest of all modalities). Detects cancers missed by mammography and ultrasound, particularly in dense breasts and high-risk women.

**Specificity**: 72–85% (lower than mammography). Higher false-positive rate leads to additional biopsies.

**Strengths**: Highest sensitivity for invasive cancer. No ionizing radiation. Superior for evaluating extent of disease, multifocality, and chest wall invasion. Best for screening high-risk women (BRCA carriers, strong family history).

**Limitations**: Expensive (5–10× cost of mammography). Requires IV contrast (gadolinium) — contraindicated with severe kidney disease. Not effective for detecting microcalcifications or DCIS. Claustrophobia can limit patient tolerance. Longer examination time (30–45 minutes).

**When recommended**: Annual screening for women with ≥ 20% lifetime risk. Evaluating extent of known breast cancer before surgery. Monitoring neoadjuvant chemotherapy response. Evaluating breast implant integrity. Screening women with prior chest radiation (ages 10–30).

## Comparison Summary

For routine screening: mammography (FFDM or DBT) is the standard of care. For dense breasts: supplemental ultrasound or MRI may be recommended. For high-risk women: annual MRI alternating with mammography provides maximum sensitivity. For young women with palpable findings: ultrasound is the initial modality. No single modality is sufficient for all clinical scenarios — multimodality imaging improves detection.
