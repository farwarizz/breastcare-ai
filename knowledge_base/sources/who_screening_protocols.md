# WHO Breast Cancer Screening and Early Diagnosis

## Global Context

Breast cancer is the most common cancer in women and one of the two most commonly diagnosed cancers overall, with about 2.3 million new cases and 685,000 deaths in 2020 (GLOBOCAN 2020). Most deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, where cancers are more often diagnosed late. Mammographic screening is the only screening method shown in randomised trials to reduce breast cancer mortality.

## WHO Position on Mammographic Screening

The WHO position paper on mammography screening (2014) recommends organised, population-based mammography screening only in well-resourced settings where the health system can guarantee quality assurance, follow-up of abnormal results and treatment. It recommends biennial screening of women aged 50–69 in such settings. Screening women aged 40–49 or 70–75 is suggested only within rigorous research or in programmes that can show their net benefit. Screening without the capacity to diagnose and treat the cancers found causes harm, through false positives, overdiagnosis and overtreatment, without the benefit.

## Recommendations by Resource Setting

**Well-resourced settings**: Organised programmes offering biennial mammography to women aged 50–69, with quality assurance, defined follow-up pathways and monitoring of performance.

**Limited-resource settings with relatively strong health systems**: Mammography screening is not recommended as the first priority. The WHO suggests investing first in early diagnosis of symptomatic disease and in treatment capacity, and considering screening only once those are in place.

**Limited-resource settings with weak health systems**: Focus on breast cancer awareness and timely diagnosis and treatment of symptomatic women. Clinical breast examination as a screening method should be offered only within research, because its effect on mortality has not been established.

## Global Breast Cancer Initiative

The WHO Global Breast Cancer Initiative (2021) aims to reduce breast cancer mortality by 2.5% per year, averting 2.5 million deaths between 2020 and 2040. It sets three targets: at least 60% of invasive breast cancers diagnosed at stage I or II (health promotion for early detection); diagnostic evaluation completed within 60 days of first presentation (timely diagnosis); and at least 80% of patients completing multimodality treatment (comprehensive management).

## Screening Effectiveness Data

In the randomised trials, inviting women aged 50–69 to mammographic screening reduced breast cancer mortality by about 20%; observational studies of women who actually attend suggest a larger reduction, around 40%. For women aged 40–49 the reduction in the trials was smaller, around 15%, with more false positives per cancer found. Screening has not been shown to reduce deaths from all causes.

## Overdiagnosis Considerations

Overdiagnosis is the detection of a cancer that would never have caused symptoms in the woman's lifetime. Estimates vary widely with the method used, from under 10% to more than 50% of screen-detected cancers. The UK Independent Review (2012) estimated that about 19% of cancers diagnosed by screening are overdiagnosed, equivalent to roughly 11% of all cancers diagnosed in invited women. Low-grade ductal carcinoma in situ accounts for a large share.

## Quality Standards for Screening Programmes

European guidelines set programme standards: double reading of mammograms by two readers; a recall rate below 7% at the first (prevalent) screen and below 5% at later (incident) screens; a cancer detection rate of at least three times the background incidence at the first screen and 1.5 times at later screens; and interval cancers below 30% of background incidence in the first year after screening and below 50% in the second. In the United States, the American College of Radiology's minimum standards for an individual radiologist include a sensitivity of at least 75% and a cancer detection rate of at least 2.5 per 1,000 examinations.

## WHO Framework for Early Diagnosis

The WHO Guide to Cancer Early Diagnosis (2017) describes three steps: awareness of symptoms and access to care; clinical evaluation, diagnosis and staging; and access to treatment. It advises that the interval from first presentation to the start of treatment should be as short as possible, with treatment starting within about a month of diagnosis and no more than about 90 days passing from first symptoms to treatment.
