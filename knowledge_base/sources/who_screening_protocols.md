# WHO Breast Cancer Screening Protocols

## Global Context

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide, with approximately 2.3 million new cases annually (GLOBOCAN 2020). The World Health Organization recognizes mammographic screening as the only proven method for reducing breast cancer mortality in organized population-level programs.

## WHO Position on Mammographic Screening

The WHO recommends organized population-based mammographic screening only in well-resourced settings with the infrastructure to ensure quality, follow-up, and treatment. Screening without adequate treatment capacity may cause harm through overdiagnosis and overtreatment.

## Recommendations by Resource Setting

**High-resource settings**: Biennial mammographic screening for women aged 50–69. Consider extending to ages 40–49 and 70–75 based on national context. Organized programs with quality assurance, double reading, and defined follow-up pathways.

**Limited-resource settings**: Clinical breast examination (CBE) as an interim screening method. Prioritize early diagnosis over screening (ensuring symptomatic women access care promptly). Invest in treatment infrastructure before expanding screening programs.

**Low-resource settings**: Focus on breast health awareness and early diagnosis of symptomatic disease. Clinical breast examination integrated into primary healthcare. Do not implement mammographic screening without treatment capacity.

## Screening Effectiveness Data

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) demonstrate a 20–25% reduction in breast cancer mortality for women aged 50–69 who participate in organized mammographic screening. Evidence for women aged 40–49 shows a smaller mortality reduction (approximately 15%) with higher rates of false positives and overdiagnosis.

## Overdiagnosis Considerations

Approximately 1 in 7 to 1 in 10 screen-detected cancers may represent overdiagnosis — cancers that would never have caused symptoms or death in the woman's lifetime. This includes low-grade ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) and slow-growing invasive cancers.

## Quality Standards for Screening Programs

Double reading by two independent radiologists (or CAD as second reader). Minimum target sensitivity ≥ 75%. Recall rate < 7% for prevalent screening, < 5% for incident screening. Cancer detection rate ≥ 5 per 1,000 for initial screening. Interval cancer rate < 30% of background incidence.

## WHO Framework for Early Diagnosis

Step 1: Awareness — population education about breast cancer signs and symptoms. Step 2: Clinical evaluation — prompt assessment by trained healthcare workers. Step 3: Access to treatment — timely referral to appropriate care within 60 days of diagnosis.
