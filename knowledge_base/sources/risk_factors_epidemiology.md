# Breast Cancer Risk Factors and Epidemiology

## Incidence and Mortality

Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer in women worldwide. GLOBOCAN 2020 estimated about 2.3 million new cases and 685,000 deaths in 2020, roughly one in four cancers diagnosed in women. In the United States, SEER estimates about 322,000 new cases of female breast cancer and 42,000 deaths in 2026, and about 13% of women will be diagnosed with breast cancer during their lifetime.

Survival depends strongly on the stage at diagnosis. In US SEER data (diagnoses 2016–2022), five-year relative survival was 91.9% overall: about 100% for localized disease (confined to the breast), 87.5% for regional disease (spread to nearby lymph nodes) and 33.8% for distant metastatic disease. Relative survival compares patients with similar women without breast cancer, so a figure near 100% means almost no excess deaths within five years.

## Age-Specific Incidence

Breast cancer risk rises with age. In US SEER data (2019–2023), the share of new female breast cancers by age at diagnosis was: under 20, 0%; 20–34, 2.0%; 35–44, 8.6%; 45–54, 17.7%; 55–64, 24.3%; 65–74, 27.5%; 75–84, 15.1%; 85 and over, 4.9%. Breast cancer is most frequently diagnosed between 65 and 74, and the median age at diagnosis is 64. About one case in ten occurs before age 45.

## Non-Modifiable Risk Factors

**Age**: After female sex, age is the strongest risk factor. Most breast cancers are diagnosed after age 50.

**Sex**: Female sex is the main risk factor. Male breast cancer accounts for under 1% of cases.

**Inherited mutations**: Women with a BRCA1 mutation have a 55–72% risk of breast cancer by age 70–80, and BRCA2 carriers 45–69%, depending on the study. TP53 mutations (Li-Fraumeni syndrome) carry a near-certain lifetime risk of some cancer, with breast cancer in about half of female carriers by age 70. PALB2 mutations raise breast cancer risk roughly five-fold, and ATM or CHEK2 mutations roughly two-fold.

**Family history**: One first-degree relative (mother, sister or daughter) with breast cancer raises risk about 1.8-fold, and two first-degree relatives about 2.9-fold. A relative diagnosed at a young age raises risk more than one diagnosed late in life.

**Personal history**: After breast cancer in one breast, the risk of a new cancer in the other breast is about 0.4% per year. Atypical ductal hyperplasia raises risk about four- to five-fold, and lobular carcinoma in situ about seven- to twelve-fold, relative to women without these findings.

**Reproductive factors**: Early menarche (first period before 12), late menopause (after 55), never having given birth, and a first pregnancy late in life each raise risk modestly, mainly through longer lifetime exposure to oestrogen.

## Modifiable Risk Factors

**Physical inactivity**: In pooled studies, the most physically active women had a 12–21% lower risk of breast cancer than the least active, for both premenopausal and postmenopausal women.

**Obesity after menopause**: Higher body mass index raises postmenopausal breast cancer risk by roughly 12% for every 5 kg/m², because fat tissue produces oestrogen through the enzyme aromatase.

**Alcohol**: Risk rises with the amount consumed, by about 7% for every 10 g of alcohol (roughly one standard drink) per day, and even light drinking raises risk slightly.

**Menopausal hormone therapy**: Combined oestrogen–progestogen therapy raises breast cancer risk from the first years of use (about 1.6-fold during years 1–4 of current use), and some excess risk persists for more than ten years after stopping.

**Smoking**: Smoking is associated with a modest increase in risk, about 10%, with the strongest associations for women who started smoking early or before a first pregnancy.

## Breast Density

The ACR BI-RADS density categories are: A, almost entirely fatty (about 10% of women); B, scattered areas of fibroglandular density (about 40%); C, heterogeneously dense (about 40%); and D, extremely dense (about 10%). Nearly half of women aged 40 and over who have mammograms have dense breasts (category C or D).

Density matters twice. Dense tissue and many cancers both appear white on a mammogram, so density lowers mammographic sensitivity (masking). Dense breasts are also an independent risk factor: compared with category B, breast cancer risk is roughly 1.4–1.6 times higher in category C and 1.6–1.8 times higher in category D.

## Lifetime Risk Categories

The average lifetime risk for a woman in the United States is about 13%. Guidelines use lifetime-risk thresholds to decide who should receive supplemental MRI screening: the American Cancer Society recommends annual MRI with mammography for women with a lifetime risk of about 20–25% or greater, recommends against MRI screening when lifetime risk is below 15%, and finds the evidence insufficient between 15% and 20%. NCCN uses a residual lifetime risk of 20% or greater, calculated with a model based on comprehensive family history, as the threshold for its increased-risk screening schedule.
