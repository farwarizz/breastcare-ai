# Breast Cancer Risk Factors and Epidemiology

## Incidence and Mortality

Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer in women worldwide, accounting for approximately 25% of all female cancers. Global incidence: approximately 2.3 million new cases per year (GLOBOCAN 2020). Global mortality: approximately 685,000 deaths per year. Five-year survival rate for localized disease exceeds 99%. Five-year survival rate for distant metastatic disease is approximately 29%.

## Age-Specific Incidence

Breast cancer risk increases with age. Incidence rates per 100,000 women by age group (approximate SEER data): Age 20–29: 1.9 per 100,000. Age 30–39: 25.1 per 100,000. Age 40–49: 122.5 per 100,000. Age 50–59: 229.9 per 100,000. Age 60–69: 350.7 per 100,000. Age 70–79: 412.3 per 100,000. Median age at diagnosis: 62 years.

## Non-Modifiable Risk Factors

**Age**: Single strongest risk factor. Risk doubles approximately every 10 years until menopause, then continues to increase at a slower rate.

**Sex**: Female sex confers approximately 100× higher risk than male. Male breast cancer accounts for < 1% of all cases.

**Genetic mutations**: BRCA1 carriers: 55–72% lifetime risk. BRCA2 carriers: 45–69% lifetime risk. TP53 (Li-Fraumeni syndrome): up to 90% lifetime risk of any cancer. PALB2, ATM, CHEK2: moderate penetrance genes conferring 2–4× relative risk.

**Family history**: One first-degree relative with breast cancer: approximately 2× relative risk. Two first-degree relatives: approximately 3× relative risk. First-degree relative diagnosed before age 50: higher risk than diagnosis after 50.

**Personal history**: Prior breast cancer in one breast increases contralateral risk by 0.5–1% per year. Prior atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH) or lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS): 4–12× relative risk. Dense breast tissue (ACR density C or D): 1.5–2× relative risk.

**Reproductive factors**: Early menarche (before age 12): slight increased risk due to longer estrogen exposure. Late menopause (after age 55): similarly increases cumulative estrogen exposure. Nulliparity or first birth after age 30: higher risk compared to early parity.

## Modifiable Risk Factors

**Physical inactivity**: Regular physical activity (≥ 150 minutes/week moderate intensity) reduces risk by approximately 10–20%.

**Obesity (postmenopausal)**: BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² increases postmenopausal breast cancer risk by 20–40%. Adipose tissue produces estrogen via aromatase enzyme activity.

**Alcohol consumption**: Risk increases linearly: each 10g/day of alcohol increases risk by approximately 7–10%. No safe threshold established.

**Hormone replacement therapy (HRT)**: Combined estrogen-progesterone HRT increases risk after ≥ 5 years of use. Risk decreases after discontinuation, returning to baseline within 5–10 years.

**Smoking**: Associated with modest increase in risk (relative risk approximately 1.1–1.3). Strongest association with premenopausal breast cancer.

## Breast Density

ACR breast density classification: Category A (almost entirely fatty): lowest risk. Category B (scattered fibroglandular): average risk. Category C (heterogeneously dense): 1.2× relative risk, masks lesions on mammography. Category D (extremely dense): 2× relative risk, significant masking effect. Dense breasts (categories C and D) affect approximately 40% of screening-age women. Density both increases cancer risk AND reduces mammographic sensitivity (masking bias).

## Lifetime Risk Categories

Average risk: < 15% lifetime risk (general population baseline ~12.9%). Moderate risk: 15–20% lifetime risk. High risk: > 20% lifetime risk (qualifies for enhanced screening with MRI).
